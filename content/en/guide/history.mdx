## Origins

The ney is a wind instrument made from a plant that grows in various lengths in wetlands. Although its origin goes back to mythological times, it is known that the Sumerians, who lived around 3000 BC, and the Aztecs, a civilization across the ocean, used this instrument. The fact that the word "ney" is found in the Divan-i Lugat-it Turk as a term for a military instrument, and that instruments resembling the ney appear in ancient Uyghur reliefs, strengthens the view that pre-Islamic Turks also used this instrument.

## The ney in the Islamic tradition

There are many accounts of the ney's origin in the Islamic tradition. The most famous is as follows: "Our Prophet told the secret of divine love to Hz. Ali. Hz. Ali, crushed under the weight of this secret, went outside Medina and told the secret to a blind well. The blind well grew agitated with this secret, frothed and overflowed. When the water covered everywhere, reeds grew on its edges. A shepherd there cut one of these reeds, bored holes in several places, and began to blow into it. The sound that came out stirred hearts with joy and excitement, and came to tell the divine secret. When our Prophet happened to hear this shepherd's ney sound, he understood what had happened. From that day on, the ney became a source of inspiration."

## Rumi and the Mevlevi order

It is Mevlana Jalal ad-Din Rumi who, in today's sense, gave the ney its soul. Holding the view that "whether Turk or Persian, music is the shared nourishment of lovers," Mevlana devoted the first eighteen couplets of his Masnavi to this captivating instrument. In his view, the ney is the guide of the whirling dervishes, who turn during the ceremony while in truth journeying through an inward realm. With the enchanted sound it produces, it draws souls in. This is why all Mevlevi dervishes — like Kutb-i Nayi Osman Dede, who trained alongside Mevlana and is regarded as the patron of ney players — have for centuries breathed in and breathed out through the ney.

## The ney at the Ottoman court

The ney received great interest at the Ottoman court. Selim III, composer of the Suz-i Dilara Ayin-i Sherif, sought innovation in music by using the ney together with the piano. Besides Mahmud II, Sultan Abdulaziz and Sultan Reshad are also known as ney-playing sultans. The ney was even popular enough in the harem for women ney players to be trained there.

Because the ney has a far more soothing quality than today's excessively loud instruments, it was also used in Ottoman Darüşşifas (hospitals) in the treatment of the mentally ill.

## Nine joints, seven holes

Technically, the ney consists of nine joints and seven holes, six of them at the front. It is believed that these nine joints are in fact inspired by the nine-jointed human throat, and the seven holes by the human head — the ears, eyes, nose and mouth.
